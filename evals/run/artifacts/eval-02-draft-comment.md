## Draft comment body

**Docket No. FDA-2024-N-2910 — Food Labeling: Front-of-Package Nutrition Information
(90 FR 5426, January 16, 2025)**

**Submitted by:** Harbor Lane Foods

### Summary of our position

We support the goal of this proposed rule. A short, standardized nutrition summary on the
front of the package would help our customers compare products quickly.

We are writing about **timing and about one gap in the economic analysis**, not about the
substance of the disclosure. We make three requests, below.

Harbor Lane Foods is a single-plant manufacturer of shelf-stable sauces and snack mixes,
with about $40 million in annual food sales and 86 active retail SKUs. We have no in-house
prepress; one co-packer runs all of our retail packaging.

### 1. Add a middle compliance tier for firms between $10 million and $100 million in annual food sales

As proposed, firms with $10 million or more in annual food sales would have three years
after the final rule's effective date to comply, and firms with less than $10 million in
annual food sales would have four years. We sit just above the $10 million line, which
places us in the same tier as the largest packaged-food companies in the country.

The three-year tier is difficult for us for reasons that have nothing to do with our
willingness to comply:

- We revise retail artwork on roughly a 30-month cycle, because that is how long it takes
  to amortize print plates across our volumes.
- Our co-packer requires about nine months of lead time to change plates across all SKUs.
- We typically hold 14 to 18 months of printed film inventory.

Together, those three facts mean a three-year clock does not give us one artwork cycle to
work with; it gives us one cycle only if the clock happens to align with it, and forces us
to scrap usable film if it does not. A four-year tier for firms between $10 million and
$100 million in annual food sales would let most firms our size fold the new label into a
revision we were already going to pay for.

### 2. Alternatively, allow compliance at the next scheduled artwork revision inside the window

If FDA prefers not to add a tier, we ask for a trigger-based option instead: permit a firm
to apply the new front-of-package information at its **next scheduled artwork revision
occurring within the compliance window**, with the end of the window as a backstop. This
preserves the deadline FDA sets, but stops the rule from forcing a mid-cycle plate change
and the destruction of printed film that would otherwise be used.

One cost point, offered as context rather than as a challenge to FDA's estimate: the
proposal uses $1,333 per product to relabel, within a Paperwork Reduction Act estimate
covering 30,413 manufacturers and 322,326 products over a three-year compliance period
($143,220,186 per year, $429,660,558 total capital cost). A 2025 quote we received for
plate and die work came to $1,450 per SKU. That is one quote to one firm and we do not
suggest it is representative. We raise it only because for a firm with 86 SKUs and no
in-house prepress, the per-product figure is the whole cost.

### 3. Estimate cost per small entity, or explain in the final rule why it cannot be estimated

Table 1 of the proposal reports total discounted cost of the proposed rule per entity,
including large firms, of approximately $100,253, and then states that FDA cannot estimate
the exact cost per small entity because it does not know how many UPCs on average are
owned by small entities under the SBA definition. The proposal also proposes to certify
that the rule will not have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small
entities, on the basis that the annual economic impact is less than 3 percent of annual
revenue.

We are not in a position to say whether that certification is right or wrong. We have not
determined our own status under the applicable SBA size standard, and we have no data on
how many UPCs a typical small manufacturer carries. That is our point: if the number of
products per small entity is unknown, the burden on small entities is unknown too.

We ask FDA to either (a) develop a per-small-entity estimate for the final rule, or (b)
state plainly in the final rule why the estimate cannot be produced and what the agency
would need in order to produce it. Either answer would help firms our size plan.

### One point on reformulation

The proposal states that reformulation is not a requirement or goal of the rule, and
attributes 68 percent of the $3.2 billion undiscounted ten-year total cost ($2.2 billion)
to voluntary reformulation, with the remaining 32 percent ($1 billion) attributable to
updating labeling. Separately, the proposal puts the present value of total costs over ten
years at $1.7 billion to $4.9 billion at a 2 percent discount rate, primary estimate $3.1
billion, and annualized total costs at $191 million to $530 million, primary estimate
$333 million in 2023 dollars over 2025–2034.

We accept that characterization as FDA's. We record our own expectation, as a firm, that
for products carrying claims such as "low sodium" the practical pressure to reformulate
rather than display a less favorable front-of-package summary will be substantial. **We
have not quantified that cost and we are not asserting a figure.** We have no
consumer-response data of our own and take no position on how shoppers will react to the
label. We raise it so the record reflects that one mid-size manufacturer expects the
voluntary category to behave as a practical requirement for part of its portfolio.

### Conclusion

We support the disclosure. We ask for a middle compliance tier, or a
next-revision trigger as an alternative, and for a per-small-entity cost estimate or an
explanation of its absence. We appreciate the opportunity to comment.
